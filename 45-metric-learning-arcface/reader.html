1. What an angular margin changes during training

Demonstration 1 of 1

What an angular margin changes during training

How does an angular margin change the target logit without changing the inference cosine?

A margin makes the true class harder to win during training: it adds to the true angle before the logits are scaled, so the same embedding gets a lower true-class logit and a higher loss. At inference the target is unknown, so no margin is applied and the cosine used for retrieval is unchanged.

Scroll sideways for the whole equation →

s is the logit scale (16 here), theta the angle between the query and its true prototype (30 degrees), m the added margin in radians, and c the fixed cosine (0.5) to a competing prototype. The inference cosine is cos(theta) and never includes m.

The margin adds 0.50 radians to the true angle (30 degrees is 0.5236 radians), so the training cosine falls: 0.8660 - 0.5203 = 0.3457. The retrieval cosine stays 0.8660. The true-class probability is 0.5805 and the loss 0.5439; the competing prototype stays at 0.5000, so the true class leads it in training.

Worked steps

  1. Inference cosine of a 30 degree angle: 0.8660, unchanged by the margin.
  2. Training cosine at 30 degrees plus 0.50 radians: 0.5203.
  3. Cosine lost to the margin: 0.8660 - 0.5203 = 0.3457.
  4. Two-class softmax with scale 16 against a competitor at 0.50: probability 0.5805.
  5. Cross-entropy: 0.5439.

Use the idea

Check whether your competition scores class prediction or identity retrieval, then compare a margin head with a normalized-softmax baseline under the same backbone and splits. Judge it on the retrieval metric, including unseen identities.

Where the conclusion applies

One forward calculation with fixed numbers: angles in radians, the true angle plus the margin stays below pi, and nothing is optimized. It shows what training demands, not tighter clusters or better retrieval.
