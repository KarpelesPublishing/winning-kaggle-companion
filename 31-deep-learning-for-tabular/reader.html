Deep Learning for Tabular Data

Judge a neural model by what it adds to the blend.

A neural model can earn a place through complementary errors even when its own score is worse. One demonstration sweeps the blend weight on four fixed rows.

1. A weaker model that still improves a blend

A weaker model that still improves a blend

Can a model with a worse individual score improve a blend through complementary errors?

Model choice and ensemble value are separate questions. Here the neural model has a slightly worse score alone but corrects the tree model on one row, while it does worse on two others. Moving the weight trades those effects against each other.

w is the weight on the neural prediction, g_i and d_i the tree and neural probabilities for row i, y_i its 0 or 1 label, n = 4 the number of rows and MSE(w) the blend's mean squared error at weight w.

At weight 0.50 the blend scores 0.0381 against the tree model's 0.0475: 0.0475 - 0.0381 = 0.0094 lower, even though the neural model alone (0.0500) is worse than the tree model. The minimum belongs to these four rows only.

Worked steps

  1. Neural weight w = 0.50, so the tree weight is 1 - 0.50 = 0.50.
  2. Blended predictions for the four rows: 0.200, 0.750, 0.200, 0.900.
  3. Blend MSE: 0.0381; tree model: 0.0475; neural model: 0.0500.
  4. Gain over the tree model: 0.0475 - 0.0381 = 0.0094.

Use the idea

Save aligned out-of-fold predictions from the new model, test a small weight grid with the metric's correct direction, and compare incremental gain with training and inference cost.

Where the conclusion applies

Four rows with two fixed prediction sets and an arithmetic blend, not neural training. The best weight is specific to these rows and this lower-is-better metric; confirm it on fresh data.

Check your understanding: At weight 1.0 what is the blend MSE, and what does that say about the endpoints?
It is 0.0500, the neural model's own MSE, and weight 0 gives the tree model's 0.0475. The curve's endpoints are the two single models.
