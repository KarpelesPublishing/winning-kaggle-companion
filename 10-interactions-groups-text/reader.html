Respecting the metric direction in feature search

If a search helper accepts candidates whose score goes up, what does it do to a candidate that lowers RMSE?

A reusable search helper must know whether larger or smaller numbers mean improvement. A larger AUC is better while a smaller RMSE is better, so one shared acceptance inequality can keep the regression features you meant to reject. Negative deltas improve every illustrative fold, positive deltas worsen them, and zero changes nothing.

b is the baseline RMSE and c the candidate RMSE, each averaged over three folds, gain is b - c, and epsilon is the tolerance 0.00001 below which a change counts as no change.

Predict first. A candidate lowers RMSE by 0.02 on every fold. A helper written for AUC accepts a candidate whose score rose. What does it do here?

Mean baseline RMSE 0.500 and candidate RMSE 0.480 give an improvement of 0.500 - 0.480 = 0.020. The maximizing rule rejects a real improvement; only the minimizing rule is correct for RMSE.

Worked steps

  1. Paired fold changes (candidate minus baseline): -0.02, -0.02, -0.02.
  2. Baseline mean RMSE: 0.500; candidate mean RMSE: 0.480.
  3. Improvement: 0.500 - 0.480 = 0.020.
  4. Maximizing rule (accept if the score rose): reject.
  5. Minimizing rule (accept if the score fell): accept.

Use the idea

Record the metric name, its direction, fixed split identities and baseline predictions before the search starts. Inspect paired fold changes to separate one unusual fold from a consistent pattern, and reserve an untouched comparison when a large search shapes the conclusion.

Where the conclusion applies

Three constructed folds with paired RMSE, smaller is better. Correct screening is necessary but not sufficient: repeatedly screening candidates on the same folds also tunes to those folds, and stepwise selection does not remove multiple-testing optimism.
