Chapter 42

Image Augmentation

Reverse each view before fusing masks.

Choose augmentations that preserve the target, and invert geometric transforms before averaging outputs. One demonstration averages a segmentation mask over two views with and without reversing the flip.

Every example is a constructed teaching example. Its results describe the declared inputs of the chapter activity, not a measured competition gain.

1. Averaging flipped masks in the right frame

Demonstration 1 of 1

Averaging flipped masks in the right frame

Why must segmentation predictions be returned to the original coordinate frame?

A segmentation mask has pixel coordinates, so a flipped view predicts the mask in a flipped frame. Averaging it with the original view as it stands mixes different pixels. Reversing the flip first puts every view in the original frame, and here that restores the mask exactly.

M_ij is the true mask value at row i and column j, and M-bar_ij the averaged prediction there. M-bar is the mean of the view predictions, taken either as predicted or after applying the inverse flip to the flipped view.

Without reversing the flip, 4 of the 6 cells are off by 0.5, so 4 x 0.25 = 1.00 summed over the cells, and 1.00 / 6 = 0.1667. Reversing the flipped prediction before averaging restores the true mask and gives 0.0000.

Worked steps

  1. True mask rows: [1.0, 0.0, 0.0] and [1.0, 1.0, 0.0].
  2. The flipped view predicts the mask with each row reversed, so its foreground sits at the other end of the row.
  3. Averaging the mask with the flipped prediction as it stands mixes different pixels: [0.5, 0.0, 0.5] and [0.5, 1.0, 0.5].
  4. Reversing the flipped prediction first, then averaging, restores [1.0, 0.0, 0.0] and [1.0, 1.0, 0.0].
  5. Unaligned: 4 x 0.25 = 1.00 summed over the cells, and 1.00 / 6 = 0.1667. Aligned MSE: 0.0000.

Use the idea

For each test-time view, apply the inverse of its geometric transform to the output before fusing. When several transforms are stacked, undo them in the opposite order, and test on an asymmetric mask.

Where the conclusion applies

A fixed two-row, three-column binary mask that the model predicts perfectly under identity and horizontal flip, so every error comes from coordinate handling. This illustrates alignment only; whether a flip preserves the task's label is a separate question.

Check your understanding: Why is the unaligned error 0 when only one view is averaged?
With the identity view alone nothing was flipped, so the prediction is already in the original frame and equals the mask.
