1. How focal gamma reweights easy and hard examples

Demonstration 1 of 1

How focal gamma reweights easy and hard examples

How does focal gamma change the relative weight of easy and hard examples?

The activity fixes three true-class probabilities, 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9, and compares cross-entropy with focal loss on each. Raising gamma lowers every loss, but it lowers the easy example far faster. At gamma 0 the two losses are identical, which makes a handy check on an implementation.

Scroll sideways for the whole equation →

p_t is the probability the model gives the true class, gamma the focusing parameter, and FL the loss for one example. At gamma 0 the factor is 1 and FL equals ordinary cross-entropy, -log(p_t).

For the hard row (p = 0.1) the focusing factor is (1 - 0.1)^2 = 0.8100, so 2.3026 x 0.8100 = 1.8651: 81.0 percent of its cross-entropy is kept. The easy row keeps 1.00 percent. The easy loss is 0.0565 percent of the hard loss, against 4.58 percent at gamma 0.

Worked steps

  1. Hard example, p = 0.1: cross-entropy 2.3026.
  2. Focusing factor (1 - 0.1)^2 = 0.8100.
  3. Focal loss: 2.3026 x 0.8100 = 1.8651.
  4. Easy example, p = 0.9: focal loss 0.001054 against cross-entropy 0.1054.
  5. Easy loss as a share of hard loss: 0.0565 percent.

Use the idea

Test that your focal loss reduces to cross-entropy at gamma 0 before training, and keep an untouched evaluation metric. A lower focal loss is a change of objective, not evidence of a better model.

Where the conclusion applies

Three fixed probabilities and no training: the table shows loss values only, not gradients or learning. Some hard rows are mislabeled rather than informative, and a larger gamma can amplify that noise.

Check your understanding: At gamma 4, how much of the middle example's cross-entropy (p = 0.5) is left?
The factor is (1 - 0.5)^4 = 0.0625, so 0.6931 x 0.0625 = 0.0433, about 6 percent of its cross-entropy.
