Turning domain probabilities into qualified weights

When one row's domain probability changes, how do the capped odds and the normalized weights of all four rows respond?

A domain classifier gives each training row a probability of looking like test data. Under the stated assumptions the odds p / (1 - p) act as a density ratio, which is capped and divided by its mean so the weights average 1. Probabilities near one make the odds, and the weight concentration, large.

The four capped odds (0.25, 0.67, 2.33, 4.00) have mean 1.813. Row 4 has the largest weight: 4.00 / 1.813 = 2.21. The weights average 1 by construction, so raising one row's odds lowers the weights of the other rows.

Worked steps

  1. Domain probabilities: 0.20, 0.40, 0.70, 0.80.
  2. Capped odds: 0.25, 0.67, 2.33, 4.00.
  3. Mean capped odds: 1.813.
  4. Row 3 weight: 2.33 / 1.813 = 1.29.
  5. Largest weight: 2.21 (row 4).

Use the idea

Cross-fit the domain probabilities, check calibration and support, cap extreme ratios and record effective sample size. Pass weights only into the training slice and compare weighted against unweighted predictions on the same held-out units.

Where the conclusion applies

The domain probabilities are calibrated and cross-fitted, the domain priors are equal, the target relationship given the features is stable and the two populations overlap. The cap of 10 is a teaching choice. The weights carry no promise of a score gain, and reweighting cannot create training examples where support is missing.

Check your understanding: Set row 3 to 0.95. The raw odds are 0.95 / 0.05 = 19. What odds and weight does row 3 get?
The cap limits the odds to 10, so the mean capped odds are (0.25 + 0.67 + 10 + 4) / 4 = 3.73 and row 3's weight is 10 / 3.73 = 2.68. Without the cap, one row would take an even larger share.
