Correcting a probability after downsampling negatives

How does negative retention change a sampled model probability?

Dropping negatives raises the share of positives the model sees, so its probabilities run high for the original population. If every positive is kept and each negative survives with probability r, the corrected odds are the sampled odds times r. A sampled 0.5 can then be a rare event.

p is the model probability, assumed calibrated to the sampled data, r the fraction of negatives kept (every positive is kept), and p_true the probability in the original population.

A sampled probability of 0.5 means odds 0.5 / 0.5 = 1. Keeping a fraction 0.1 of the negatives scales the odds: 1 x 0.1 = 0.1, so the corrected probability is 0.1 / (1 + 0.1) = 0.0909. Order is preserved, but the scale shifts a lot.

Worked steps

  1. Retention r = 0.1. Each corrected value is p x r / (1 - p + p x r).
  2. Sampled 0.1: 0.01 / 0.91 = 0.0110.
  3. Sampled 0.5: 0.05 / 0.55 = 0.0909.
  4. Sampled 0.9: 0.09 / 0.19 = 0.4737.

Use the idea

Record the retention probability when you downsample and choose one fix: this analytic correction, or calibration on a representative sample. Never apply both, and score the corrected probabilities with the competition metric.

Where the conclusion applies

All positives kept, negatives sampled independently with one probability r, and sampled probabilities calibrated to the sampled distribution. If retention varies with features, or a calibrator already restored the original distribution, this correction is wrong.

Check your understanding: With r = 0.5, what does a sampled probability of 0.9 become?
The sampled odds are 0.9 / 0.1 = 9, times 0.5 gives 4.5, and 4.5 / 5.5 = 0.8182.
